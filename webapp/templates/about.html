About the Model

SPRINGS-ABM simulates diarrheal disease transmission and household response in a community modeled on Akuse, Ghana. This page explains what it does and why, in plain language first, with a technical drill-down under each section for anyone who wants the mechanics. The trust table further down is the same information that powers every info box on the scenario builder - one place to check how much weight any given number should carry.

What this model is

The model follows thousands of individual simulated people, living in households, going about daily life in a place built from the real geography of Akuse - its roads, water sources, schools, and places of worship. Two diarrheal pathogens circulate through this population: Rotavirus, which spreads person-to-person and through contaminated water, and Campylobacter, which spreads mainly through contact with household animals as well as food and water. When a child falls ill, their parent has to decide - day by day - whether it's worth paying for medical care, a decision shaped by how much the household can afford and how the parent personally weighs risk. That decision feeds back into the outbreak itself: care that's sought and paid for clears the illness faster, so who can afford care - and who can't - shapes how the epidemic unfolds, not just how any one family fares.

Technical detail

Formally, this is an SVEIR (Susceptible-Vaccinated-Exposed-Infectious-Recovered) compartmental model, simulated at the individual-agent level rather than as population-level equations, coupled to a household economic model and a Cumulative Prospect Theory (CPT) behavioral-decision layer for care-seeking. All three layers run inside one shared daily time step, so effects propagate between them: epidemic dynamics shape household wealth (via lost income and paid care), and household wealth shapes care-seeking, which shapes epidemic dynamics.

Who's represented

Every simulated person belongs to a household - most households have 3-4 members, generated so that every household has at least one adult. Children under five are the model's central concern: they're the ones who get sick, and their illnesses are tracked in detail (severity, duration, whether care was sought). Adults can carry and spread both pathogens too, but in this model they never show symptoms themselves - a simplification worth keeping in mind when interpreting results. In every household with children, one adult is the "parent" who makes care-seeking decisions - each with their own individual mix of risk tolerance and loss aversion, so two otherwise-identical households can respond very differently to the same sick child.

Technical detail

Household sizes are drawn from a Poisson distribution (mean 3.2); the first member of every multi-person household is always an adult, and every subsequent member independently rolls a chance of being a child. Illness onset (severity, duration, health toll) is gated to is_child agents only - adults can be infected and shed pathogens into water, but never take a health toll from it. Each parent's decision-making uses Cumulative Prospect Theory: loss aversion (λ), probability distortion (γ), and a wealth/health utility weight (α) are drawn per-agent from 32 sampled behavioral archetypes, so heterogeneity in risk-taking is built in from the start rather than assumed away.

How illness spreads, and how families respond

Rotavirus spreads two ways: directly between people, and through a shared water source that can become contaminated and infect anyone who uses it. Campylobacter spreads mainly through contact with household-owned poultry or livestock, with smaller contributions from household-level fecal-oral contact and food. When a child falls ill, severity and expected duration are set at the moment of infection based on the pathogen, the child's age, and how many times they've been sick before - repeat infections tend to be milder, but immunity is never complete, which is a big part of why these diseases keep circulating rather than dying out. Each day the illness continues, the parent faces the same choice again: seek paid care, which usually cures the child but costs money the household may not have, or wait it out, risking the illness getting worse.

Technical detail

Severity is computed once at onset (age x immunity x pathogen base severity) and held fixed for the episode; duration is a simple day-by-day countdown. Immunity from prior infections is asymptotic, never reaching zero susceptibility or zero severity - the mechanistic reason the model sustains transmission indefinitely in a closed population with no births. Care-seeking is evaluated via CPT: the parent compares the near-certain cost of care against the uncertain, worse-if-untreated cost of waiting, with outcomes weighted by their individual persona parameters rather than by expected value alone.

The environment

The simulated town isn't an abstract grid - it's built from real OpenStreetMap data for Akuse, Ghana, so the water sources, schools, and places of worship agents travel to and from are the actual ones on the ground there. There's exactly one version of this map; every scenario you run uses it. What does change from run to run is the simulated population living on it - who's related to whom, where households are placed, and each person's individual behavioral traits - controlled by the random seed on the scenario builder. On top of this fixed geography, the model layers occasional water-contamination events (a burst pipe, a fouled well) that temporarily raise infection risk - how often these happen is one of the scenario builder's adjustable levers.

Technical detail

The spatial grid is a content-hashed, pre-built asset (identified by a grid ID) loaded once per run; there is currently no procedurally-generated randomness in the grid itself, so re-creating it would reproduce the same file byte-for-byte. Water-contamination "shocks" are modeled as a daily probability applied to the shared water reservoir's contamination state, with a separate, independent recovery probability governing how quickly a contaminated source reverts to clean.

How much to trust each number

Not every parameter in this model carries the same weight. Some are drawn directly from published literature; some were fit ("calibrated") so the model's output matches a real benchmark, like a national health survey; some are documented, reasonable assumptions the model needs to run but that haven't been independently validated; and a few are purely structural, like which spatial grid to load. Known limitations

  • Adults are silent carriers. They can be infected and transmit both pathogens, but the model never gives them symptoms, a health toll, or a care-seeking decision of their own - so total burden on adults is understated by design, not by oversight.
  • One fixed geography. Every scenario runs on the same real map of Akuse; the model doesn't yet represent other locations or let the physical environment itself vary between runs, only the population living on it.
  • Household economics use abstract units. Wealth, income, and cost of care are fractions of a household's maximum wealth, calibrated to reproduce a real behavioral outcome (the care-seeking rate) - not real currency amounts, and shouldn't be read as such.
  • No births, deaths from other causes, or population turnover. The simulated population is closed and fixed in size for the duration of a run.
